What Fair Sky Network does, in plain English

You don't need to know anything about aerospace or blockchain. By the end of this page you'll understand the problem, see the product working in real screenshots, and know why people pay for it.

1

The whole ideaOne sentence

Chinese factories sell billions of dollars of jet-engine metal to Boeing, Airbus, and their suppliers. The paperwork proving that metal is real is a PDF anyone can fake. Fair Sky Network makes a forged certificate worthless — and traces every claim to the person who signed it. The factory pays us to prove they're real.

5

The "aha"The magic trick: a number that checks itself

Here's the thing we learned from looking at real aerospace paperwork. A single melt of metal gets a batch number — say BLH0421. As that metal moves from the mill, to the forge, to the customer, a human being literally re-types that same number onto each company's separate PDF. That hand-typed number is the only thing linking the documents together.

Today · paper

A worker copies BLH0421 from the mill's cert onto the forge's cert onto the customer's cert.

If someone fakes one document and types a real-looking number, nobody notices for years — until an airplane part fails.

With Fair Sky Network

That number becomes a live link. The forge's credential automatically reaches back and checks the mill's credential.

If the link doesn't match a real, signed record, it fails instantly — right there on the screen the inspector is holding.

We didn't invent the chain of custody. We just made the paperclip un-fakeable.

7

The honest limit"What if someone swaps the metal itself?"

The sharpest question we get — usually from a quality engineer. If a fraudster takes a real certificate and ships different metal under it, no digital system alone can catch that. Here's what changes anyway — and how the gap gets closed:

1
The fraud gets a name attached. Today, swapped metal plus fake paper is anonymous and deniable. With Fair Sky Network, the hijacked credential was signed by a real company at a recorded moment — when bad metal surfaces, investigators know exactly which signed shipment it claimed to be, and the signer can prove what they actually shipped. Deniable fraud becomes attributable fraud.
2
The 30-second gun check. Metal receiving docks already use handheld XRF guns (X-ray fluorescence — point it at the metal, get its elemental recipe in seconds). Today that reading gets compared to paper that might itself be fake. With Fair Sky Network it's compared to the signed credential — and the check is recorded onto the chain of custody.
3
Random lab audits. Independent labs periodically test real samples against what the supplier signed. One failed audit and the supplier loses the badge. Like anti-doping in sports: you don't test everyone every day — you make cheating too risky to be a business model.
4
Year 2: binding the atoms. Laser-etched serials and stored metal "fingerprints" tie each physical piece to its credential — so a certificate can't be reused on different metal at all.

We don't claim to stop every fraud. We make fraud attributable, detectable, and expensive — a crime with a name attached, instead of a business model.

The businessWhy it makes money — and why it's defensible

The factory pays. Not the customer. The factory is the one who needs to prove it's trustworthy to win and keep Western orders — so it subscribes. The customer verifies for free, forever, which is what makes the whole thing spread.

Our software is open source — which sounds backwards until you see the split:

Free & open

  • The code that checks a certificate
  • The certificate format
  • The customer's verification page

What we sell

  • Being the trusted registry of who's real
  • Running the service the factories rely on
  • The independent lab spot-checks
  • The brand customers learn to trust

The code is free. Being the trusted referee is the business.

Same model as Red Hat (sold for $34B), MongoDB, and Sentry. Open code builds trust; the hosted, branded, verified service is what people pay for.
